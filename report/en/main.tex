\begin{abstract}
Electricity-price forecasts in this project serve a constrained daily scheduling decision: choose one eight-slot charging block followed by one eight-slot discharging block, or remain idle. We begin with a failure of the original evaluation logic: lower pointwise RMSE does not reliably identify higher realized dispatch value among the saved candidates. We explain this through the geometry of the feasible actions and reformulate learning as maximizing the realized value of the action induced by a forecast. The implementation combines centered block-sum regression, a listwise window loss, and a frozen five-seed price-curve average. On the specified 59-day evaluation period, this protocol realizes a mean score of \textbf{6693.88}, compared with \textbf{6214.36} for the historical MSE-trained Transformer reference: an observed gain of \textbf{479.51 (7.72\%)}. Window-ranking consistency has a stronger descriptive association with value than point error, whereas the training surrogate itself does not show a stable association. The gain is concentrated: 86.0\% of its net total arises on one day, the paired seven-day block interval is $[-227.87,1626.28]$, and the headline records a deeper single-day loss than the reference in the same window. The contribution is a traceable failure-driven formulation and implementation with a positive period-specific outcome; transferable improvement and loss-specific causality remain open.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} electricity-price forecasting; predict-then-optimize; constrained scheduling; learning to rank; ensemble evaluation.

\section{Introduction}
The practical question is simple: \emph{does a more accurate price curve produce a better charging and discharging decision?} The answer matters because a forecasting system is ultimately judged by the decisions it enables. Pointwise error weights every forecast deviation, while a fixed scheduling policy depends on a small number of differences between feasible window values. Better performance on the first criterion therefore need not order finite fitted models in the same way as the second.

The historical point-prediction candidates examined here were evaluated by squared error. A subsequent replay of the same saved price curves through the scheduling solver exposed a mismatch: a contextual Transformer with worse point error could produce a higher realized score. That observation motivates the central progression of this report: define the original learning problem, identify the decision structure it only indirectly serves, reformulate the objective, implement a tractable training surrogate, and evaluate the resulting frozen protocol.

The report makes three bounded contributions. First, it connects the observed failure to the action-contrast directions that determine regret. Second, it implements block-level regression and listwise learning over all 3321 active windows, followed by curve averaging across five fixed seeds. Third, it reconciles prediction provenance, reports the observed improvement, and separates descriptive gain decomposition from causal attribution. These are contributions to the formulation and evaluation of this particular task; no new general learning theorem or universal superiority of a loss is claimed.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Price forecasting and point accuracy.}
Reviews of electricity-price forecasting emphasize model comparisons, evaluation protocols, and reproducible benchmarks \citep{lago2021}. Tree methods such as LightGBM provide strong tabular baselines \citep{ke2017}. Here the supplied target is an anonymized price series, and these references provide methodological context rather than evidence that the data originate from a particular electricity market.

\paragraph{Learning for downstream optimization.}
Predict-then-optimize research evaluates forecasts through the decisions they induce. Smart Predict-then-Optimize introduces decision-aware losses and the SPO+ surrogate \citep{elmachtoub2022}. This motivates distinguishing price error, predicted decision value, and realized decision value. Our main implementation does not differentiate through the discrete solver; it trains a smooth surrogate on the finite set of active window values.

\paragraph{Ranking and ensembles.}
\citet{mandi2022} formulate decision-focused learning through ranking losses. We adapt a listwise construction with separate target and prediction temperatures and a centered block-sum error term. Independently initialized model ensembles also have a substantial literature \citep{lakshminarayanan2017}. Our use is specifically to average price curves before selecting one legal action; it does not establish calibrated predictive uncertainty. The task-specific synthesis and its audited evidence are the focus.

\section{Problem Setup: A Failure-Driven Formulation}

\takeaway{The original objective specifies what price errors to minimize, but does not directly optimize the value of the induced schedule.}

\subsection{Step 2: define the actual downstream decision}
\EqDispatch
There are $\sum_{c=0}^{80}(81-c)=3321$ active schedules and one idle schedule. Every active schedule charges at the fixed coded power for eight consecutive slots, then discharges for eight slots, with zero output elsewhere. Denote a solver-selected maximizer by $u^*(q)$, using the recorded deterministic tie rule. The realized score is $S(A,q)=A^\top u^*(q)$; the oracle is $O(A)=\max_{u\in\mathcal U_{8+8}}A^\top u$.

The solver sees $q$, and settlement sees $A$ only after the action has been chosen. The official project score is the unscaled sum of price times coded power. It does not multiply by 0.25 hours and is reported in original score units, not currency or net trading profit.
\takeaway{Forecast quality matters through the feasible action it selects. Predicted value and realized value are different quantities.}

\subsection{Step 3: identify the empirical failure}
\EqFailure
These two points use the saved contextual Transformer curves from the v1 and v2 candidate sets on the same 59 days. Both RMSE and realized score are recomputed or verified against those same curves. The lower-error curve realizes less value. The example refutes a monotone ordering of these fitted candidates; differing recipes mean it is not an isolated experiment on one model parameter. Section~\ref{sec:results} supplies the complete candidate comparisons.
\takeaway{The failure is an unreliable model-ranking proxy in this project, not evidence that squared-error learning is universally wrong.}

\subsection{Step 4: explain why mismatch is possible}
\EqInvariance
Every action in $\mathcal U_{8+8}$ has $\mathbf1^\top u=0$. A common price offset therefore cancels, and a positive rescaling preserves all window orderings and the idle threshold. The argmax sets are identical; a fixed tie rule also gives the same action in exact arithmetic. The code takes the first active window within $64\varepsilon_{64}\max_w|S_w|$ of the maximum, ordered by charging then discharging start, and selects idle if that score is nonpositive. This treats machine-level near ties as ties, so exact algebraic invariance is not a guarantee of identical floating-point actions under arbitrary extreme rescaling. RMSE generally changes under either transformation. This invariance relies on the balanced action contract and must be reconsidered when losses, costs, or unequal energy flows are added.

\EqRegret
The following regret bounds concern exact maximizers; numerical tie handling and audit tolerances are separate. Here $u_A=u^*(A)$, $u_q=u^*(q)$, and $\Pi_{\mathcal D}$ is the orthogonal projection onto the space of feasible action differences. Optimality under $q$ gives $q^\top(u_A-u_q)\le0$, yielding the first upper bound. The second follows from projection and Cauchy--Schwarz. Forecast errors perpendicular to every action contrast cannot change relative action values. Even within the relevant subspace, the same error norm may occur near or far from a boundary between competing actions.
\takeaway{Decision regret depends on errors along feasible action contrasts and on decision margins. A smaller full-curve error norm does not by itself order candidate realized values.}

\subsection{Step 5: reformulate the learning objective}
\EqOuter
The outer problem chooses a predictor to maximize the actual value of the schedule it will induce; the inner problem selects a legal schedule using only its predicted prices. Replacing maximization by expected oracle regret gives an equivalent objective because the oracle term does not depend on the chosen predictor. True training labels may supervise learning, and evaluation labels may settle a locked action, but neither permits future labels to enter a live decision.

\EqBayes
This identity is an essential qualification. With finite second moments and unrestricted population squared-error learning based on $X$ and a fixed feasible set, the conditional mean is optimal for the risk-neutral linear objective among decisions using $X$. Our motivation is therefore finite data, restricted models, regularization, optimization, and possible distribution change---not a contradiction of this population result. Equation~\eqref{eq:outer} is also risk-neutral; it contains no explicit tail-risk penalty.
\takeaway{The objective is reconstructed to match the task under practical learning restrictions. A smooth surrogate is an implementation choice, not an automatic guarantee of better decisions.}

\subsection{Step 6: distinguish the implemented contract from battery physics}
\EqPhysical
Here $c_t,d_t$ are physical charging and discharging powers, $s_t$ is state of charge, $E_{\max}$ is battery capacity, and $\eta_c,\eta_d$ are efficiencies. The set $\mathcal U_{8+8}^{\rm phys}$ expresses the same window strategy in physical power units. The battery and strategy constraints must both hold. A terminal condition such as $s_{96}=s_0$ would be an additional modeling choice; it is not implied by the displayed constraints.

For a purely illustrative battery with capacity 4 MWh, initial SOC 50\%, charging power 1 MW, and unit efficiency, eight quarter-hour charging slots add 2 MWh and reach 100\% SOC, violating the 90\% bound. Thus legal windows alone do not ensure physical feasibility. Equal charging and discharging blocks also do not restore the initial SOC when efficiency is below one.
\takeaway{The reported 6693.88 was obtained under $\mathcal U_{8+8}$ only. Explicit SOC, capacity, efficiency, cycling costs, and network constraints are an extension, not implemented features of this result.}

\subsection{Map prices to the quantities the solver reads}
\EqBlocks
The centering operator $C$ removes the daily level. Here $C\in\mathbb R^{96\times96}$ and $B\in\mathbb R^{89\times96}$ produces 89 overlapping eight-slot sums, with $g(q)\in\mathbb R^{3321}$. Each active action value is the difference of two block sums, multiplied by $P$. The bound in~\eqref{eq:blockbound} follows because every active action has at most $2P\|BC(A-q)\|_\infty$ value error, and regret compares two actions. It motivates attending to block errors, but does not say that minimizing block MSE exactly minimizes regret.

Some historical block models output block \emph{means}. Their corresponding sums are eight times those means. This constant preserves ordering but changes loss scales and softmax temperatures; the present loss uses sums and omits the common score multiplier $P=1000$.

\subsection{From a hard optimal-window label to a soft ranking target}
\EqHardLoss
The original window loss couples a block-value anchor with cross-entropy on one oracle window. Code computes the hard-label argmax from uncentered true prices: centering cancels between equal-length blocks, preserving the maximizing set in exact arithmetic. The tensor argmax selects the first exact maximum; this is distinct from the dispatch solver's near-tie tolerance. A wrong first-ranked window can still be near optimal in realized value, and ties can make a unique index an arbitrary label. We therefore use a soft target over all active window values:
\EqListwise
The target temperature controls how much probability near-optimal windows retain. The prediction temperature controls logit sharpness. These are fixed recipe values in the final ensemble, not parameters tuned on this report's diagnostics. The true-price target is detached from gradient computation. The discrete argmax remains in evaluation; training differentiates through block sums and softmax.

As $\tau_{\rm tgt}\to0$, the target tends to the uniform distribution on the maximizing set. It becomes the one-hot hard-window target only when the maximizer is unique, with the other settings held equal. This is a relation between loss families, not proof that the selected listwise recipe dominates the earlier loss.

Fixed-temperature cross-entropy is sensitive to positive rescaling of $q$, even though the action is invariant. The block term anchors amplitude, but neither term makes the total loss a scale-invariant decision metric. We explicitly test the unchanged training proxy in Results and report its weak association. In addition, the loss covers active windows only: learning when to choose idle is not explicitly supervised by its categorical target.

\subsection{Freeze the recipe and average curves before optimizing}
\EqMean
Averaging commutes with evaluating a \emph{fixed} action because the objective is linear. It does not commute with argmax. Averaging legal powers could violate the fixed 8+8 strategy; averaging curves followed by the exact solver returns one permitted action. The final pipeline freezes the family, loss parameters, epoch count, five seeds, and curve-mean combiner before this ensemble evaluation. Other saved combinations remain diagnostic controls.

SPO+, pairwise, and differentiable black-box alternatives belong to the broader exploratory record. The central method here is the stated listwise recipe plus curve averaging, not a claim that every decision-focused route improves performance.

\subsection{Provenance and statistical procedure}
The analysis verifies 62 saved prediction curves across five source directories: common timestamps and targets, legal saved actions, predicted objective optimality, actual settlements, and oracle values. Plots of point candidates use RMSE recalculated from their saved prediction arrays. Six historical RMSE entries differ from the curves associated with their settlements; both values are retained separately in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}. No source leaderboard is edited.

We use paired circular day-block resampling with primary block length seven days, 10,000 replicates, and fixed random seed $20260915+7$. Sensitivities use block lengths one and three with correspondingly offset seeds. Each replicate samples contiguous circular blocks until 59 positions are obtained. All models share the sampled days, and metrics and candidate correlations are recalculated together. This preserves pairing and some short-range dependence; it assumes the observed sequence can represent comparable periods. Intervals use the 2.5\% and 97.5\% bootstrap percentiles. \textbf{Only dates are resampled; the 16/19 candidates remain fixed. Candidate sampling, model search, and training randomness are excluded.} These intervals do not correct historical exploration or guarantee population coverage under future seasonality or distribution shift.

\subsection{R2: What does the reconstructed objective actually explain?}
\EqDiagnostic
\reportfigure{fig03_proxy.pdf}{Two different diagnostics on the same candidate curves. Top: the actual headline training proxy with fixed parameters from Table~\ref{tab:config}. Bottom: the scale-invariant window-ranking statistic $T$. This figure tests the surrogate rather than presuming it works; the bottom row measures decision structure, not a replacement training objective validated on future data.}{fig:proxy}
The unchanged listwise training proxy correlates with realized value at $+0.320$ in v1 and $-0.165$ in v2. Since lower loss is preferred, the desired negative association is neither stable nor strong. We therefore do not claim that the new training loss is a superior cross-model selection metric.

Window-ranking consistency is different: its Pearson association with realized value is $+0.849$ in v1 and $+0.777$ in v2, with conditional intervals $[0.472,0.881]$ and $[0.197,0.852]$. Average ranks handle tied window values. The primary definition uses float64 eight-slot sums; changing reduction order alters a few machine-level ties but leaves three-decimal conclusions unchanged. Controlling linearly for the ratio of centered forecast amplitude to centered true amplitude yields partial correlations $0.824$ and $0.713$ (intervals $[0.448,0.868]$ and $[0.142,0.807]$). Applying the same amplitude control to the training proxy gives $0.059$ and $-0.361$, with intervals $[-0.443,0.335]$ and $[-0.664,0.023]$. Thus both panels receive the same sensitivity analysis, which still does not establish stable surrogate selection ability. Linear control is not causal adjustment and cannot remove all model differences.

Window rank and realized value are functions of the same forecast/true window-value vectors: the former measures all window orderings, while the latter reads true value at the predicted argmax. Their high association may partly reflect this mathematical coupling. They are not identical statistics, and the observed magnitude remains data-dependent. This is a structural diagnosis, but it does not show that validation-period surrogate rankings predict later performance. In particular, computing the empirical outer objective $J$ on these same days would simply reproduce realized score. A genuine selection test requires predictions on an earlier period excluded from all fitting. The v2 checkpoint-fitting pipeline uses all 301 development days; v1 sequence models fit on 287 and early-stop on the remaining 14, also overlapping val30. Metadata do not enumerate fit dates, so this scope must be traced through split records and fitting code. These checkpoints cannot directly provide val30 evidence untouched by development. The window-rank intervals are markedly asymmetric (v1 bootstrap median about 0.771). We use them as sensitivity ranges, not calibrated small-sample coverage, and do not apply ordinary IID BCa to dependent days.
\takeaway{The data support the relevance of window ordering. They do not yet validate the stronger claim that the trained surrogate selects better future models.}

The same-initialization convention applies only within each model family in the recorded Codex control run, not across all historical routes. Its window-CE Transformer control realizes 6570.71 as a single seed, above the listwise single-seed mean of 6375.35 and its best recorded seed, 6551.77. These recipes also differ in epoch count (29 versus 13), loss settings, and selection history, so this is a counterexample to an across-record dominance claim, not a causal test proving the older loss superior. A corresponding LSTM control is 137.79 below its earlier reported result, illustrating sensitivity to implementation and initialization conventions without isolating their individual effects.
\takeaway{The evidence isolates a within-recipe arithmetic averaging increment and a selection-rule control. It does not isolate a universally superior loss or a transferable ensemble effect.}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{What the theoretical analysis contributes}
The useful abstraction is the composition of a forecast with a constrained optimizer. The action set determines which price contrasts matter; the learning objective determines how finite model capacity is allocated to them. This explains why error diagnostics should be derived from the decision contract and why changing the feasible set may require redesigning the loss. The regret bounds connect that intuition to explicit inequalities, while the conditional-mean result prevents overstating the failure of MSE.

\EqVariance
If errors in a particular action-value contrast had equal variance $\sigma^2$ and common pairwise correlation $\rho$, averaging would have the displayed variance. This is a possible mechanism for stabilizing relative action estimates. Neither $\sigma^2$ nor $\rho$ is estimated here, and lower contrast variance does not guarantee higher value after a discontinuous argmax. The observed concentration on one date makes this a mechanism hypothesis, not an established explanation of the gain.

\subsection{Limits of the evidence and the next discriminating tests}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time and prior exposure.} There are only 59 evaluation days, previously observed in the wider research process. Heavy tails, dependence, and historical exploration limit prospective interpretation; day-block intervals do not remove these limitations.
\item \textbf{Physical and information scope.} Battery SOC, losses, degradation, and network limits are absent from the implemented solver. The availability time of the full input forecast curve and the data's market provenance require external confirmation before deployment.
\item \textbf{Surrogate scope.} The actual fixed-temperature proxy is not scale invariant and has weak candidate-level associations here. Its active-only target also omits the idle alternative, which could matter in low-opportunity periods.
\item \textbf{Attribution.} The headline differs from the reference in training recipe and seed aggregation. Existing controls do not form a complete same-family, same-initialization, same-budget factorial loss experiment.
\item \textbf{Outcome distribution and downside.} The outer business objective is risk-neutral expected score; actual training uses MSE or block/ranking surrogates and does not directly optimize expected realized score; Equation~\eqref{eq:outer} carries no tail penalty. In this window the headline's worst day is $-5922.66$ against $-2340.48$ for the reference. With 59 heavy-tailed days the future frequency of such losses cannot be estimated reliably. If the application constrains single-day downside, that constraint belongs in the feasible set or the objective and requires re-evaluation.
\end{itemize}
Three future tests would discriminate the remaining claims: evaluate frozen protocols across new rolling periods; compare validation RMSE and surrogate rankings using predictions excluded from fitting; and compare losses under matched family, initialization, training budget, selection rule, and seed count. These tests are proposed, not performed in this report.

\section{Conclusions}
Failure analysis showed that pointwise error did not reliably rank the realized value of the saved scheduling candidates. Starting from the legal action set, we expressed this mismatch through invariance and action-contrast regret, reformulated the learning objective, and implemented a block/listwise surrogate with frozen curve averaging. The protocol realizes 6693.88 against 6214.36 on the specified period, an observed gain of 479.51. Its concentration, downside, and uncertainty are integral to that result. The general lesson is to design and evaluate learning around the decisions and constraints it serves, while separating a plausible mechanism, a realized outcome, and evidence of future improvement.
